# 1. Executive conclusion

For a live instructional event, the slides are **not the lesson, not the script, not the handout and not the presenter**.

Their primary jobs are to:

1. control and direct audience attention;
2. make invisible/complex information visible;
3. externalise structure and relationships that are hard to hold in working memory;
4. provide evidence for a spoken assertion;
5. signal what matters **now**;
6. support retrieval, decision and comparison;
7. reduce repeated operational instructions during practice;
8. create visual continuity across a long event.

For the Formalife Full Course, the hierarchy should be:

**live instructor / live demonstration / learner action -> relevant visual evidence -> minimal on-slide words -> notes/reference layer.**

The deck should never compete with the instructor or the manikin for attention.

The strongest general research base comes from Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning / cognitive-load research and converges on:

**coherence -> signaling -> contiguity -> segmentation -> complementary verbal/visual channels -> active retrieval/generative activity.**

A 2022 overview of 29 reviews covering 1,189 studies and 78,177 participants found positive meta-analytic support for multiple multimedia-design principles, with especially robust evidence for spatial/temporal contiguity, signaling, modality, animation in appropriate contexts, coherence/removing seductive details and segmentation. Good design mattered particularly for complex and system-paced material — exactly the situation of a live four-hour course.

Core implication:

**the harder the material and the less control the learner has over pace, the more disciplined the slide design must be.**

# 2. Evidence hierarchy — what is strong, contextual or merely craft convention

## 2.1 Strong / broadly supported

Use as default doctrine:

- remove irrelevant material (`coherence`);
- cue what matters (`signaling`);
- put labels next to what they describe (`spatial contiguity`);
- show the visual when it is being explained (`temporal contiguity`);
- do not project long text that duplicates narration (`redundancy/modality boundary`);
- break complex information into meaningful stages (`segmenting`);
- pre-establish key concepts before complex procedures (`pre-training`);
- use relevant words + relevant pictures rather than words alone (`multimedia`);
- make learners retrieve, predict, explain or decide rather than only watch (`generative activity / testing effect`).

## 2.2 Good evidence but context matters

Use deliberately, not automatically:

- animation instead of static graphics;
- emotional/aesthetic design;
- assertion–evidence slide structure;
- progressive disclosure;
- video;
- advance organisers / section maps.

## 2.3 Weak as universal rules / mostly heuristics

Do **not** turn into Formalife dogma:

- `7 × 7`, `6 × 6` or similar word-count commandments;
- `one slide per minute`;
- `10/20/30 rule`;
- a fixed maximum number of slides;
- “always use dark background” or “always use white background”;
- “animations are bad” or “animations keep attention”;
- arbitrary transition effects;
- a magical 10–15 minute human attention limit.

Use rehearsal, venue and learning job instead.

# 3. The semantic unit of a good slide

## 3.1 Default explanatory slide = message, not topic

For explanatory/evidence slides, the best default is the **Assertion–Evidence** structure:

**sentence headline = what the audience should conclude**

+

**visual evidence = why that conclusion is credible/understandable.**

Supporting detail lives primarily in speech/notes.

Research comparing assertion–evidence with traditional topic + bullet slides has found improved comprehension/recall for more complex technical concepts; a 2026 primer on didactic presentations recommends the same structure.

Therefore avoid generic headings such as:

- `Trauma cranico`
- `Cause`
- `Rianimazione`
- `Statistiche`

when the slide is making a teaching claim.

Prefer meaningful assertions such as:

- `Quasi l’86% dei ricoveri della serie italiana riguardava bambini sotto i 4 anni`
- `Pila a bottone, magneti e oggetti appuntiti cambiano subito il livello di urgenza`
- `Nel bambino le ventilazioni fanno parte della risposta, non sono un optional`

The sentence should usually be **one conclusion**, not the entire explanation.

## 3.2 Assertion–Evidence is a default, not a religion

Several Formalife slide families have a different semantic job and should **not** be forced into sentence-assertion format.

Valid exceptions:

### Retrieval slide

Job: withhold answer.

Example:
`Qual è la sequenza?`

The assertion appears only after retrieval.

### Scenario slide

Job: provide evidence/cues but **not** answer.

Example:
`Bambino di 2 anni; possibile pila a bottone; sta bene.`

### Algorithm / decision slide

Job: externalise a sequence/branch.

The semantic centre is the decision map itself.

### Practice-hold slide

Job: reduce operational friction.

Example:
`Tentativo -> correzione -> ripetizione -> scenario -> senza prompt.`

### Narrative / emotional bookend

Job: frame meaning, not prove a technical claim.

### Break / operational state

Job: return time / logistics.

Formalife rule:

**choose the slide grammar from the learner’s job, not from a style template.**

# 4. What belongs on the slide vs in the speaker

## 4.1 On the slide

Put information on screen when the audience benefits from **seeing it while hearing the explanation**:

- the main conclusion;
- a photograph that is evidence/context;
- a diagram of mechanism;
- a visual contrast;
- a graph or numerical relationship;
- a physical landmark;
- a decision fork;
- an ordered procedure;
- a key number;
- a short quote only when the exact words matter;
- a short instruction during practice;
- a retrieval prompt;
- scenario facts;
- source/year for statistics.

## 4.2 In the instructor’s voice/notes

Keep off-screen when it does not need to be visually inspected:

- complete explanations;
- long caveats;
- contextual detail;
- transitions and story;
- examples that can be spoken in one sentence;
- legal conditions;
- the presenter script;
- scientific nuance that does not alter the learner action;
- fallback answers to likely questions;
- instructor-only timing/technical instructions.

## 4.3 In Guide / Pack / post-course reference

Move out of the live deck when the content is important for later lookup but does not justify live visual attention:

- comprehensive symptom lists;
- complete reference algorithms;
- detailed follow-up conditions;
- full citation/bibliography;
- medical subtleties beyond the first-action contract;
- detailed policy conditions.

This is particularly important for Formalife because customers already receive a Guide + Pack. The deck therefore does **not** need to function as a printable textbook.

# 5. The redundancy problem — do not make people read the presenter

The visual and verbal channels have limited capacity. Projecting the same complete sentences the instructor is saying forces the learner to process redundant verbal information visually and auditorily while also trying to integrate graphics.

Recommended Formalife rule:

**if the speaker can say it, the slide should usually show something else that helps the audience understand it.**

Good combination:

- instructor explains;
- slide shows diagram / photo / chart / concise labels.

Bad combination:

- instructor reads paragraph;
- identical paragraph is projected;
- decorative image sits beside it.

Exception:

short exact wording worth remembering can appear on screen while spoken, e.g.:

- `FERMA -> VALUTA -> AGISCI`
- `5 ventilazioni -> 30:2 -> DAE`
- one anchor sentence;
- a question.

Accessibility caveat:

captions can be necessary even where redundancy theory would otherwise prefer no duplicated text. Accessibility requirements take precedence for users who need captions.

# 6. Coherence — beautiful does not mean busy

Research on `seductive details` consistently warns against interesting but instructionally irrelevant additions. A 2026 multilevel meta-analysis of 50 studies / 177 effects found a small but significant negative effect of seductive details on overall learning, recall, comprehension and transfer, primarily through increased extraneous cognitive load.

A study specifically testing decorative PowerPoint animations found worse recall and higher mental workload than static images.

Formalife therefore should **not** use:

- decorative GIFs;
- background motion;
- floating medical icons;
- random stock photography merely to “fill space”;
- decorative animations because a slide feels static;
- ambient music under instructional content;
- visual flourishes unrelated to the message;
- fake dashboard decoration around simple data;
- repeated logos/watermarks that compete for attention.

Important nuance:

visual quality and aesthetics still matter. Emotional-design research suggests attractive materials can support motivation and sometimes performance. The correct distinction is:

**aesthetic structure that improves clarity/coherence = good**

versus

**interesting decoration that competes with learning = bad.**

Whitespace is not unfinished space. It is attention control.

# 7. Signaling — show the eye where to look

A meta-analysis of 103 studies / 12,201 participants found signaling beneficial for retention and transfer and associated with reduced cognitive load.

Formalife should actively use visual cueing:

- one accent colour for the element currently discussed;
- dim/de-emphasise non-current elements;
- arrows only when they communicate a real relation/direction;
- circle/highlight one anatomical landmark;
- direct labels instead of a remote legend;
- bold/weight to identify a key word;
- progressive reveal of steps;
- visual grouping/proximity;
- consistent icons for `FAI`, `NON FARE`, `112`, `ATTENZIONE`.

Do not signal everything.

If five elements are highlighted, none is highlighted.

# 8. Spatial and temporal contiguity

## Spatial

Place labels close to the visual object they identify.

Prefer:

`arrow + “epiglottide” beside the structure`

over

`number 4 in diagram -> lookup number 4 in legend 20 cm away`.

On a chart, direct-label the line/bar where possible instead of forcing the audience to repeatedly consult a legend.

## Temporal

Reveal/show the relevant visual **when** it is discussed.

Do not put the entire complex procedure on screen and then explain step 1 while steps 2–8 compete for attention.

Formalife translation:

**the visual state of the slide should track the current sentence of the instructor.**

# 9. Progressive disclosure / builds

Progressive disclosure is one of the most useful PowerPoint techniques when it is used to **segment complexity**, not to decorate.

Use builds when:

- an algorithm has several sequential steps;
- a state changes;
- the learner must predict before seeing the answer;
- a chart needs one relationship highlighted at a time;
- anatomy is being constructed/deconstructed;
- one comparison would be cognitively overloaded if shown all at once.

Good sequence:

1. show context;
2. make learner prediction;
3. reveal first meaningful element;
4. explain;
5. reveal next;
6. integrate final state.

Use `Appear` / very short `Fade` by default.

Avoid:

- fly-in;
- bounce;
- spin;
- zoom merely for spectacle;
- random entrance directions;
- effects that make the audience wait for PowerPoint.

Craft heuristic, not research law:

**a normal reveal should feel almost instantaneous (~0–0.4 sec), because the learner should notice the information, not the animation effect.**

# 10. Static images, illustrations and diagrams

## 10.1 Use a static photograph when the photograph itself is evidence

Best for:

- real environment/context;
- food-preparation comparisons;
- scenario setting;
- product/material recognition;
- showing real Formalife practice/proof later where appropriate.

Requirements:

- high resolution;
- crop aggressively to the relevant subject;
- avoid generic stock “happy family” photography on technical slides;
- use one dominant photo rather than collage unless comparison is the message.

## 10.2 Use illustration when reality contains too much noise

Best for:

- airway anatomy;
- hand position;
- hidden internal structure;
- causal mechanism;
- simplified action sequence.

Medical illustration often beats photography when the learning job is to isolate a landmark or causal relationship.

## 10.3 Use diagrams when relationships are the content

Good diagram answers:

- what causes what?;
- what comes next?;
- what changes if state X appears?;
- who does what?;
- how do two states differ?

Avoid “infographic” diagrams containing 20 icons and 12 boxes. A diagram should reduce inference work, not add it.

# 11. Data visualisation

Data slides must answer a question, not merely display numbers.

## 11.1 Prefer perceptually accurate encodings

Research in graphical perception shows comparisons based on **position/length on a common scale** are generally more accurately read than angle, area or colour saturation.

Therefore Formalife default:

- bar / dot / position charts > pie/donut when accurate comparison matters;
- direct labels > separate legends;
- one highlighted value/series > rainbow palette;
- remove non-informative gridlines/borders;
- retain axis/denominator/context needed to prevent misleading interpretation.

## 11.2 Headline states the implication

Bad:
`Distribuzione per età`

Better:
`Quasi l’86% dei ricoveri della serie italiana riguardava bambini sotto i 4 anni`

The chart then proves/explains the headline.

## 11.3 Do not animate charts for spectacle

Animation can be useful to introduce series/causal stages one at a time.

Do not animate bars growing simply because PowerPoint can.

## 11.4 Observed vs estimated data

Formalife-specific rule from ACT 1:

observed hospital data and estimated broader-event burden must have **different visual encodings**, not merely different footnotes.

Examples:

- solid vs hatched;
- measured vs estimate label;
- separate panels.

# 12. Animation — when motion is information

Meta-analyses find a small overall advantage for instructional animation over static graphics, with stronger effects when animation is representational, realistic/video-based or supports procedural-motor learning.

But animation is not automatically better. It creates transient information that may disappear before the learner integrates it.

Formalife use animation when the learner benefits from seeing **change over time**:

- airflow / obstruction mechanism;
- transition from effective to ineffective cough;
- simple causal progression;
- a moving hand/position only if live demo cannot show the relationship clearly;
- change from one decision state to another.

Do not use animation merely because:

- slide feels empty;
- presenter wants more energy;
- it looks premium;
- an icon can move.

## 12.1 Cue animated motion

If a dynamic visual contains multiple moving elements, use cues/spotlight/zoom carefully to guide attention to the one that matters.

## 12.2 Segment complex motion

Prefer:

**play -> pause -> explain -> continue**

or discrete stages over one long continuous animation.

---

# 13. GIFs

Default Formalife rule:

**avoid looping GIFs.**

Why:

- motion continues while the instructor moves on;
- loops capture attention repeatedly;
- pacing is not controlled by teaching needs;
- decorative animations have evidence of increasing extraneous load and impairing recall.

A GIF is acceptable only when the **loop itself is the phenomenon to inspect**, e.g. a very short repeated mechanical movement.

Even then, preferred implementation is often:

- convert to controlled video/animation;
- play 1–2 times;
- pause on a diagnostic frame.

---

# 14. Video

Video should be used when **real motion / temporal behaviour is evidence that a static image cannot carry efficiently**.

Good Formalife video jobs might include:

- a 5–15 sec airway/physiology animation if superior to a diagram;
- a difficult-to-see micro-technique if the live demonstration cannot give every seat a good view;
- a brief real scenario/proof clip in a future mature version where it directly teaches something.

For core choking/CPR technique, **live demonstration remains primary** because:

- learners can inspect the real trainer/manikin;
- instructor can control pace;
- question/correction is immediate;
- participant practice follows directly.

Therefore do not replace the live demos with long videos.

## 14.1 Video production rules

- one learning job per clip;
- short and tightly edited;
- no intro sting/logo animation;
- no background music unless it is actually instructional;
- signal where the learner should look;
- introduce the viewing question before playback;
- after playback, retrieve/interpret — do not immediately continue speaking over the next slide;
- embed locally in the PowerPoint, not web-stream during a paid live course;
- test audio/video offline before the event;
- captions where speech is meaningful and accessibility requires it.

Online-learning research shows engagement falls sharply as videos lengthen; the exact `6 minute` online rule should **not** be copied into a live course, but it supports the general direction: use brief targeted clips rather than mini-documentaries.

Formalife live default:

**seconds, not minutes**, unless a video is genuinely the central learning object.

# 15. Slide transitions

There is not strong evidence that fancy slide-to-slide transitions improve learning.

Therefore Formalife default:

- **Cut/None** for normal semantic change;
- **very short Fade** when a softer visual transition is desirable;
- **Morph** only when continuity of the *same object* across two states carries meaning, e.g. a diagram reorganising from normal airway to obstructed airway or a state map zooming into one branch.

Never use transition variety as entertainment.

Avoid:

- push/wipe chosen randomly;
- cube/flip/gallery;
- transition sound effects;
- long cinematic movements between every slide.

Semantic rule:

**if the transition itself does not communicate a relationship, make it invisible.**

# 16. Program / long-deck organisation

A 4h10 system-paced course requires explicit orientation.

Research on signaling/signposting and advance organisers supports making structure visible.

Formalife should use three levels of navigation.

## Level 1 — macro ACT structure

Audience should understand the broad journey but **not** see a dense 8-item agenda every ten minutes.

At opening, a minimal narrative path may be useful:

`capire il problema -> avere un metodo -> imparare le skill -> applicarle -> portarle a casa`

This is more meaningful than `ACT 0 / ACT 1 / ACT 2...` for customers.

## Level 2 — local signposting

At major transitions, tell learners:

- where we are;
- what problem this next part solves;
- what will be different at the end.

After the main break, reorient briefly before ACT 4.

Before ACT 5, explicitly announce the changed grammar/rhythm.

Before ACT 6, announce `no new content; now application`.

## Level 3 — persistent micro-grammar where it reduces load

ACT 5 is the clearest example:

`COS'È | PREVIENI | RICONOSCI | FAI | NON FARE | ESCALA`

The topic changes; the cognitive map remains stable.

## Progress bar?

Do not add a permanent progress bar merely as decoration.

Consider a very subtle progress marker only if rehearsal shows participants lose orientation over 4h10. If used, it should show **meaningful sections**, not slide count.

# 17. Preview -> Chunk -> Recap

A 2026 didactic-presentation primer recommends explicit organisation and `Preview–Chunk–Recap` to help learners stay oriented.

Formalife translation:

### Preview

What is the decision/skill we are about to solve?

### Chunk

Teach one coherent element.

### Recap/retrieval

What is the one change/decision to remember?

This should operate primarily at the ACT/sub-block level, not become a repetitive formula on every slide.

Examples already compatible with the course:

- ACT 3: recognition -> child -> infant -> practice -> outcome;
- ACT 4: sequence -> demo -> QCPR -> reconstruct sequence;
- ACT 5: topic grammar + anchor line;
- ACT 6: scenarios -> mirror test -> debrief;
- ACT 7: three anchors -> continuity.

# 18. Typography

Research/practice consensus is clearer on **legibility** than on one perfect typeface.

Use:

- familiar sans serif;
- regular/medium/bold weights;
- generous spacing;
- left alignment for multi-line text;
- sentence case.

Avoid:

- condensed fonts;
- ornate/display fonts for instructional content;
- ALL CAPS paragraphs;
- long italics;
- underlining except links/special purpose;
- thin/light text over projection.

Microsoft recommends at least 18 pt and larger for distant rooms; recent didactic guidance recommends at least 24 pt; Assertion–Evidence guidance commonly uses ~28 pt headline / 18–24 body as minimums.

For Formalife, because the deck is live and does not need to be a handout, recommended production target is more conservative:

- **headline / core assertion: 34–44 pt minimum target**;
- **main labels/body: 28–32 pt target**;
- **chart/diagram labels: 22–28 pt target**;
- **source footer: 16–18 pt only if not part of the primary reading task; full reference can live in hidden appendix**.

These are venue-tested targets, not absolute laws.

Final test:

**read from the worst seat in the actual room.**

---

# 19. Colour and contrast

Use colour as **functional signal**, not paint.

Recommended system:

- one dominant neutral background system;
- one Formalife brand accent;
- a small semantic palette for states such as `azione`, `attenzione`, `errore/non fare`, `112/emergenza` if needed;
- neutral grey for context that is not currently important.

Accessibility:

- WCAG minimum target: **4.5:1** for normal-size text, **3:1** for large text;
- do not rely on colour alone to communicate meaning;
- pair colour with label, icon, shape, position, line style or pattern.

For graphs:

wrong:
`red line vs green line` only.

better:
`direct label + different line style/marker + colour`.

Do not use emergency red for every important element. If everything is urgent, nothing is.

# 20. Layout and visual hierarchy

The audience must know what to look at in ~1–2 seconds.

Default hierarchy:

1. assertion/question;
2. one dominant visual/evidence object;
3. one secondary callout if needed;
4. source/footer.

Use alignment, size, proximity and whitespace before adding boxes/lines.

Formalife rule:

**each slide gets one visual centre of gravity.**

Avoid:

- four equal panels unless comparison truly requires them;
- dashboards;
- cards-within-cards aesthetic;
- multiple competing headline sizes;
- icon grids where one diagram would be clearer;
- text centred in long multi-line blocks.

---

# 21. Images and text overlays

Text over photography is acceptable only when:

- the image has a naturally quiet area;
- contrast remains strong;
- the image is background/context, not evidence requiring inspection.

If the image contains evidence, do not cover the evidence with text.

Prefer:

- image on one side + assertion on clean field;
- or full image + minimal label outside the evidence area.

---

# 22. Icons

Icons work when they are a **stable vocabulary**.

Good Formalife uses:

- 112;
- `FAI`;
- `NON FARE`;
- timer;
- phone;
- AED;
- observe/monitor;
- hazard.

Bad use:

- unique decorative icon for every bullet;
- mixing multiple icon styles;
- icons whose meaning requires explanation;
- icon + label + illustration repeating the same semantic content.

The icon set should be consistent in stroke/geometry and tested at projection size.

---

# 23. Questions and retrieval slides

A question slide should **withhold the answer**.

Do not place the expected answer in a diagram beside the question.

Use:

- blank branch;
- scenario image;
- simple prompt;
- enough facts to decide.

Then reveal the answer only after commitment.

This applies strongly to:

- A0 baseline;
- FVA retrieval;
- `tosse efficace o inefficace?`;
- CPR sequence retrieval;
- ACT 6 scenarios;
- ACT 7 final anchors.

---

# 24. Blank the screen when the screen is not the teacher

When attention must be on:

- the instructor’s hands;
- the manikin;
- a volunteer;
- the room discussion;
- a real QCPR display;

the deck should become neutral, dim or black if the current slide competes.

This is especially important during:

- full-speed choking demo;
- Complex Mirror Test;
- moments of physical correction;
- Q&A/clarification unrelated to current visual.

PowerPoint should be used as an **attention switch**, not a permanent illuminated billboard.

---

# 25. Live demos vs prerecorded media

For physical skills, the evidence on animation/video supports dynamic representation, especially for procedural-motor learning — but Formalife has something better than a prerecorded representation: **a live instructor + physical trainer + immediate practice**.

Therefore hierarchy:

1. live demo;
2. static landmark/sequence visual supporting the live demo;
3. short animation/video only when it reveals something the room cannot see live.

Do not add video simply because other high-end courses use video.

---

# 26. PowerPoint operating doctrine

For paid live delivery, reliability matters more than novelty.

Recommended technical direction:

- 16:9 widescreen;
- use Slide Master / layout system;
- embed all essential video/audio locally;
- no internet-dependent media;
- use SVG for icons/diagrams where possible;
- use high-resolution raster images for photos;
- avoid screenshotting charts if chart can be rebuilt cleanly;
- keep a local PDF fallback for static emergency use;
- test on the actual presentation computer/projector;
- use Presenter View for notes/timing;
- keep unique slide titles in the file for accessibility/navigation, even where the title is visually hidden;
- check reading order and accessibility;
- run PowerPoint Accessibility Checker before release;
- test every build/animation manually with the remote/clicker;
- no hover/online interactions that require a mouse in live delivery.

Already assertion/anchor-like and suitable.

Best candidates:

- choking recognition branch;
- CPR algorithm;
- 112 two-rescuer/single-rescuer branch;
- ACT 5 do/don’t/escalate states;

## 27.3 Preserve separate pages when the audience’s job changes

Do **not** collapse merely to reduce slide count when the learner mode changes from:

- listen -> predict;
- observe -> practice;
- practice -> debrief;
- scenario -> answer reveal;
- commercial/operational -> emotional close.

## 27.4 Likely motion candidates in the current deck

### Strong candidate

`A3-03 — airway mechanism`

### Moderate candidate

`A4-03 — 5 breaths -> 30:2 -> AED`

### Strong no-video cases

- child/infant choking demo: live instructor;
- CPR demo: live instructor;
- ACT 5 topics: mostly static/diagram;
- ACT 6 scenario: static contextual image + live cues.

### Avoid GIFs throughout V1

No current learning job requires an uncontrolled looping GIF.

# 29. Slide QA checklist before rehearsal

For every slide/state ask:

## Semantic

- What is the **one job**?
- What should the participant conclude/do differently after seeing it?
- Is the title a topic or a meaningful message?
- Is this slide needed at all?

## Cognitive

- Does anything irrelevant compete for attention?
- Is the instructor saying the same text the audience is reading?
- Can information be segmented/revealed?
- Are related labels and visual elements spatially adjacent?
- Does the visual appear exactly when discussed?

## Visual

- Can the back row read it?
- One centre of gravity?
- Sufficient contrast?
- Does colour have a redundant non-colour cue?
- Is the visual evidence actually relevant?
- Are labels readable without zooming?

## Dynamic

- Does animation show a real change or just movement?
- Would static be clearer?
- Can the audience pause cognitively between stages?
- Does the transition communicate relationship?

## Live event

- Where should the participant be looking: screen, instructor, manikin, partner or own hands?
- If not screen, should we blank/dim it?
- Does the slide reveal an answer before the learner commits?
- Does it save instructor time or create more explanation?

# 30. Venue / rehearsal tests

The first deck rehearsal should include specific slide tests:

1. **back-row test** — all instructional text/labels readable from farthest seat;
2. **3-second hierarchy test** — viewer can identify main message/visual immediately;
3. **grayscale / colour-blindness check** — meaning survives without colour;
4. **bright-room test** — contrast still works;
5. **demo-attention test** — screen does not steal gaze from instructor/manikin;
6. **build timing test** — click/reveal happens at exact teaching sentence;
7. **remote/clicker test** — no animations requiring mouse precision;
8. **offline media test** — videos and fonts work without network;
9. **source/footer test** — enough provenance without unreadable clutter;
10. **cognitive-overload test** — if instructor has to say “non guardate tutto, guardate solo qui”, redesign the slide.

# 31. Formalife provisional design doctrine from the research

Unless founder/rehearsal evidence changes it, the next deck-design pass should start from these rules:

1. **Slides are an attention-control layer, not notes.**
2. **One cognitive job per slide/state.**
3. **Explanatory slides default to assertion + visual evidence.**
4. **Question/scenario slides deliberately withhold the answer.**
5. **Show visuals and say words; do not project the speech.**
6. **No decorative visuals or decorative animation.**
7. **Use progressive disclosure to segment, not entertain.**
8. **Use animation/video only when motion itself teaches.**
9. **No uncontrolled GIFs in V1.**
10. **Live demonstration beats prerecorded demonstration for core physical skills.**
11. **Put labels where the learner looks; reveal them when needed.**
12. **Charts communicate one inference and use accurate visual encodings.**
13. **Use colour as signal, never as the only code.**
14. **Typography is large enough for the worst seat, not the designer’s laptop.**
15. **Use whitespace and hierarchy instead of boxes and decoration.**
16. **Long course structure is signposted, not constantly re-agenda’d.**
17. **Blank/dim the screen when the screen is no longer the learning object.**
18. **Commercial/operational visuals disappear before the final bookend.**
19. **PowerPoint runtime must be offline-reliable.**
20. **Rehearsal, not aesthetics, decides final slide count.**

# 32. Key research / practice sources reviewed

## Multimedia learning / cognitive load

- Fiorella, L. & Mayer, R. E. (2021), `Principles for Reducing Extraneous Processing in Multimedia Learning`, Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning — coherence, signaling, redundancy, spatial/temporal contiguity.
- Mayer, R. E. & Fiorella, L. (2021), `Principles for Managing Essential Processing in Multimedia Learning` — segmenting, pre-training, modality.
- Mayer, R. E. (2022), `Multimedia Learning`, Oxford Handbook of Educational Psychology.
- Mayer, R. E. (2017), `Using multimedia for e-learning`, Journal of Computer Assisted Learning.
- Noetel et al. (2022), `Multimedia Design for Learning: An Overview of Reviews With Meta-Meta-Analysis`, Review of Educational Research — 29 reviews, 1,189 studies, 78,177 participants.
- 2025 meta-analysis of Mayer’s multimedia-learning corpus (`Educational Review`, DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2025.100730) — reinforces boundary-condition thinking rather than treating every principle as universal.

## Signaling / segmentation

- Schneider et al., `A meta-analysis of how signaling affects learning with media` — 103 studies, N=12,201; positive retention/transfer effects and reduced cognitive load.
- Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016), signaling text-picture relations meta-analysis.
- Rey et al. (2019), `A Meta-analysis of the Segmenting Effect`, Educational Psychology Review.

## Seductive details / decoration

- Rey (2012), review/meta-analysis of seductive-detail effect.
- Cheng et al. (2026), multilevel meta-analysis of seductive details — small negative effects on recall/comprehension/transfer through extraneous load.
- Pink & Newton (2020), `Decorative animations impair recall and are a source of extraneous cognitive load`.
- Hellier & Sperring (2023), review of seductive details in electronic slide presentations.

## Animation / dynamic visuals

- Höffler & Leutner (2007), animation vs static meta-analysis; larger effect for procedural-motor learning.
- Berney & Bétrancourt (2016), animation meta-analysis, overall small positive effect with meaningful moderators.
- Amadieu et al. (2011), cueing supports animation comprehension and can reduce extraneous load.
- Hasler et al. (2007), learner-controlled/segmented animation improves performance for complex material.

## Video

- Mayer (2021), `Evidence-Based Principles for How to Design Effective Instructional Videos`.
- Brame (2016/2017), `Effective Educational Videos` — cognitive load, engagement, active learning; signaling, segmenting, weeding, matching modality.
- Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014), 6.9M MOOC video sessions; shorter targeted videos produced higher online engagement. Used only as directional evidence, not a direct live-course timing rule.

## Slide structure / Assertion–Evidence

- Garner & Alley (2013), `How the Design of Presentation Slides Affects Audience Comprehension`.
- Garner et al. (2011), `Assertion-evidence slides appear to lead to better comprehension and recall of more complex concepts`.
- Penn State Assertion–Evidence research/tutorial.
- Harvard Catalyst / Harvard T.H. Chan slide-design and assertion–evidence guidance.
- 2026 primer `Developing, designing, and delivering more effective research and didactic presentations` — one key point, sentence assertion, visual evidence, oral supporting detail.

## Structure / signposting

- 2026 didactic-presentation primer — explicit organisation, signposting and Preview–Chunk–Recap.
- advance-organiser meta-analytic literature — generally positive effects on learning/retention; used as support for structural orientation, not as justification for dense agenda slides.

## Data visualisation

- Cleveland & McGill graphical-perception research and subsequent reviews — position/length generally more accurately perceived than angle/area/colour.
- 2023 CHI review/collation of graphical-perception findings.

## Accessibility / typography

- Microsoft PowerPoint Accessibility guidance — large sans-serif fonts, sufficient contrast, unique slide titles, logical reading order, video captions/accessibility.
- Microsoft `Tips for creating and delivering an effective presentation`.
- WCAG 2.2 / W3C — contrast, do not use colour alone.
- Harvard T.H. Chan slide design checklist.
